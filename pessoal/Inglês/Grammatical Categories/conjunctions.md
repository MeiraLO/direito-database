# Conjunctions — C1 Advanced (CAE) Guide

## What Is a Conjunction?
A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses. At C1 level, the focus shifts from simple connectors (*and*, *but*) toward subordinators and correlatives that express precise logical relationships — heavily tested in the Use of English and Writing papers.

The conjunction is not important to the frase, but when you use it, it is to unite two phrases. If you use it, you need two phrases. (*When* I went to the market) doesn't make sense without anything else like (I saw an elephant)
## 1. Coordinating Conjunctions
Join elements of equal grammatical rank.

| Conjunction | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| For | reason (formal) | She left early, *for* she had an early flight. |
| And | addition | He studied law *and* worked part-time. |
| Nor | negative addition | She didn't call, *nor* did she write. |
| But | contrast | It was expensive, *but* worth it. |
| Or | alternative | Submit the form *or* email it. |
| Yet | contrast | He was exhausted, *yet* he kept working. |
| So | result | It started raining, *so* we left. |

> *For* and *nor* are rare in speech but common in formal written register — useful for C1 essays.

## 2. Subordinating Conjunctions
Introduce a dependent clause linked to a main clause.

**Time:** when, while, before, after, since, as soon as, until, once, as long as
- *As soon as* the verdict was announced, the courtroom fell silent.

**Reason / Cause:** because, since, as, given that, seeing that
- *Given that* the evidence was inconclusive, the case was dismissed.

**Contrast / Concession:** although, though, even though, while, whereas
- *Although* the contract was signed, it was never enforced.
- *While* and *whereas* are ideal for balanced, formal contrast: *Civil law relies on codified statutes, whereas common law relies on precedent.*

**Condition:** if, unless, provided (that), providing (that), as long as, on condition that, even if, in case
- The appeal will proceed *provided that* the deadline is met.
- *In case* + present tense expresses precaution, not a hypothetical: *Take an umbrella in case it rains* (≠ *if it rains*).

**Purpose:** so that, in order that (formal)
- The judge adjourned the session *so that* the parties could negotiate.

**Result:** so...that, such...that
- The ruling was *so* controversial *that* it sparked nationwide debate.

## 3. Correlative Conjunctions
Work in fixed pairs.

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| both...and | The decision affects *both* the plaintiff *and* the defendant. |
| either...or | You can *either* appeal *or* accept the ruling. |
| neither...nor | *Neither* the witness *nor* the lawyer arrived on time. |
| not only...but also | The law is *not only* outdated *but also* poorly enforced. |
| whether...or | It's unclear *whether* the clause applies *or* not. |

> C1 tip: *Not only* at the start of a sentence triggers inversion: *Not only did the court reject the appeal, but it also imposed a fine.*

## 4. The Classic C1 Trap: Conjunction vs. Preposition
This distinction appears constantly in Use of English Parts 1 and 4.

| Type | Followed by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Conjunction | a clause (subject + verb) | although, even though, while |
| Preposition | a noun / pronoun / gerund | despite, in spite of |

- ✅ *Although* it was raining, we went out.
- ✅ *Despite* the rain, we went out.
- ❌ ~~Despite it was raining, we went out.~~
- ❌ ~~Although the rain, we went out.~~

The same logic applies to *because* (conjunction) vs *because of* (preposition):
- *Because* the trial was delayed... / *Because of* the delay...

## Common Mistakes

**General**
- Confusing *despite/in spite of* (+ noun) with *although* (+ clause).
- Combining *but* and *although* in one sentence (redundant): ~~Although it was late, but I stayed.~~
- Opening formal essays with *so* or *and* (fine informally, avoided in CAE Writing).

**For Portuguese speakers**
- Bilingual dictionaries often translate *"apesar de"* as both *despite* and *although*. Learners sometimes choose *although* but keep the *apesar de* + noun structure: ❌ "Although the rain, we went out" → ✅ **Despite the rain...** / **Although it was raining...**
- *"Embora"* already requires a full clause in Portuguese (similar to *although*), so once the conjunction/preposition split is clear, this pattern is usually easy to apply correctly.

## Quick Practice
1. ___ the judge's warning, the lawyer continued to interrupt. (Although / Despite)
2. The hearing was postponed ___ the main witness was unavailable. (because / because of)
3. ___ did the company breach the contract, ___ it withheld payment. (not only...but also)
4. We will proceed ___ both parties agree to mediation. (unless / provided that)
5. The statute is broad, ___ its application is narrow. (so / whereas)

**Answers:** 1. Despite 2. because 3. Not only / but also 4. provided that 5. whereas
