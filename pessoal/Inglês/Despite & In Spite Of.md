
`Despite` and `in spite of` have the **same meaning** and follow the **same grammar rules**. Both introduce a contrast — something surprising or unexpected given what follows.

---

## The Core Rules

1. They have the same meaning and follow the same grammar rules.
2. They can **never** be followed by another clause (i.e., not directly by subject + verb).
3. They can **never** be followed by a subject.
4. They **must** be followed by an **object (noun/pronoun) or a gerund (-ing)**.

**Structure:** `despite / in spite of + noun / pronoun / gerund`

- **Despite the rain**, we went to the hearing.
- **In spite of the delay**, the petition was accepted.
- **Despite feeling** exhausted, she finished the brief.
- **In spite of him** being late, the meeting went ahead. _(possessive/object pronoun + gerund — common in formal English)_

---

## What They Cannot Do

❌ **Cannot be followed by a subject + verb (a full clause):**

- ~~Despite it rained, we went out.~~
- ~~In spite of she was tired, she finished the work.~~

✅ **Correct alternatives**, if you want to use a full clause, switch to **although / even though / though**:

- **Although** it rained, we went out.
- **Even though** she was tired, she finished the work.

If you want to keep `despite`/`in spite of` with a clause-like idea, turn the clause into a noun phrase or gerund:

- Despite **the rain**, we went out. _(noun)_
- Despite **being tired**, she finished the work. _(gerund)_

---

## The "despite the fact that" Workaround

Sometimes you have two full clauses and want to keep the exact contrast without changing the wording into a noun or gerund. In that case, use **"despite/in spite of the fact that"** — this creates an object (**the fact**) for `despite` to attach to, and the `that`-clause simply describes that object.

**Original two clauses:**

> I didn't study, but I passed the exam anyway.

**Rewritten with despite:**

> I passed the exam, **despite the fact that** I didn't study.

❌ **We cannot say:**

> ~~I passed the exam despite I did not study.~~

**Why this fails:** `I` is a **subject**, and `despite` can never be directly followed by a subject — it needs an object. `Despite I did not study` tries to attach a full clause (subject + verb) directly to `despite`, which breaks the core rule above.

**The fix:** insert **"the fact that"** to manufacture an object for `despite` to take. `The fact` is the noun object; `that I didn't study` is just a clause describing _which_ fact — grammatically, it's no different from saying _despite the rain_ or _despite the delay_.

|Clause needed|Fix|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Two full clauses (subject + verb)|Insert **"the fact that"**|I passed, **despite the fact that** I didn't study.|
|Same idea, different structure|Switch to **although/even though**|**Although** I didn't study, I passed.|
|Same idea, reduced to noun/gerund|Use **despite + noun/gerund**|Despite **not studying**, I passed.|

## Why "Despite" Is Never Followed by "Of"

This is one of the most common errors for Portuguese speakers, and it has a clear explanation: **the "of" is already built into the meaning of `despite`.**

- `Despite` comes from Old French/Latin roots meaning "in contempt of" or "notwithstanding" — the idea of "of-ness" (the connection to the following noun) is already grammatically absorbed into the word itself. `Despite` behaves as a **complete preposition** on its own, so it takes its object directly, just like other single-word prepositions (_during_, _without_, _against_).
- `In spite of`, by contrast, is a **three-word prepositional phrase**: _in_ + _spite_ (a noun, meaning "malice" or "defiance") + _of_ (linking the noun `spite` to its object). Here, `of` is doing necessary grammatical work — it connects the noun `spite` to whatever follows, the same way `of` works in _"in front of the building"_ or _"in charge of the case."_

So the confusion happens because learners often blend the two structures — adding `of` to `despite` by analogy with `in spite of`, since they share a meaning. But grammatically:

|Expression|Type|Needs "of"?|
|---|---|---|
|**despite**|single-word preposition|❌ No — "of" is already implied|
|**in spite of**|prepositional phrase (in + noun + of)|✅ Yes — "of" links "spite" to the object|

❌ ~~Despite of the rain, we went out.~~ ✅ **Despite** the rain, we went out. ✅ **In spite of** the rain, we went out.

## Common Errors to Watch For

- ~~Despite of~~ → **Despite** / **In spite of**
- ~~Despite he was tired~~ → **Despite being tired** / **Although he was tired**
- ~~In spite the rain~~ → **In spite of the rain** _(don't drop the "of" from the three-word phrase)_
- ~~I passed the exam despite I did not study~~ → **I passed the exam despite the fact that I did not study.** _(a subject like "I" can never follow despite directly)_
