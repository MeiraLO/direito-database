**Structure**: has/have + been + verb-ing

**Use**: Focuses on the **duration** or **ongoing nature** of an action. Either it's still happening now, or it just stopped and its process (not just result) is relevant.

**Examples**:

- "Sana has been talking to them." → Emphasis on the ongoing activity — she may still be talking, or she just finished a long conversation.
- "I have been writing the report." → Emphasis on the process — you've been working on it, maybe it's not finished yet.
- "She has been visiting Paris a lot lately." → Focus on a pattern of ongoing activity, often implying it may continue.

#### Key differences at a glance

|Key differences|Present Perfect Simple|Present Perfect Continuous|
|---|---|---|
|**Emphasis**|Result / completion|Duration / process|
|**Is it finished?**|Yes, typically|Maybe still ongoing|
|**Countable actions?**|Works well ("visited three times")|Doesn't work with countable numbers|
|**Example**|"I have read the book." (it's done)|"I have been reading the book." (still in progress, or just paused)|
#### A useful test

If you can naturally add "for two hours" or "all day" and it sounds right, continuous usually fits better:

- "I have been talking to them **for an hour**." ✓ (continuous — natural)
- "I have talked to them **for an hour**." — less natural; simple tends to pair with completed, discrete facts rather than duration.

One more nuance: with certain verbs (know, believe, own, want — stative verbs), you generally can't use the continuous form at all, even if duration is implied: "I have known her for years" (not "have been knowing").
