# Future Tenses

English doesn't have a single "future tense" the way it has a past simple — instead there are several future _forms_, and choosing the right one is about **meaning and attitude**, not just grammar. This is exactly the kind of nuance Cambridge Use of English and Writing reward at C1.

---

## 1. Overview — which form, when

|Form|Structure|Main use|
|---|---|---|
|will + infinitive|will + base verb|Predictions, decisions made at the moment of speaking, promises, offers|
|going to + infinitive|am/is/are going to + base verb|Plans/intentions decided before now, predictions based on present evidence|
|Present continuous|am/is/are + -ing|Fixed arrangements (usually with a time/place already set)|
|Present simple|base verb (+s)|Timetables, schedules, official/fixed events|
|Future continuous|will be + -ing|An action in progress at a specific future point; polite/neutral enquiries|
|Future perfect|will have + past participle|Something completed _before_ a future point|
|Future perfect continuous|will have been + -ing|Duration of an action up to a future point|
|Future in the past|was/were going to; would|A plan made in the past (often not carried out)|

---

## 2. will vs. going to — the core distinction

This is the single most common source of errors, because Portuguese doesn't force this distinction the way English does.

**will** — decided _at the moment of speaking_, or a prediction based on opinion/general belief:

- _I'm thirsty. I'll get some water._ (spontaneous decision)
- _I think Brazil will win the next World Cup._ (opinion-based prediction)
- _I'll help you carry that._ (offer)
- _I promise I'll call you tonight._ (promise)

**going to** — decided _before_ the moment of speaking (a plan/intention), or a prediction based on present evidence you can see/point to:

- _I'm going to study law at UFF._ (decided in advance — a plan)
- _Look at those clouds — it's going to rain._ (evidence visible right now)
- _She's going to hand in her thesis next month._ (already arranged)

**Quick test:** if you can insert "I've already decided..." or "based on what I'm seeing right now..." before the sentence and it still makes sense, it's _going to_. If the idea only occurred to the speaker as they spoke, it's _will_.

---

## 3. Present continuous vs. going to (for plans)

Both can express future plans, but:

- **Present continuous** implies a fixed arrangement — a specific time, date, or place is usually attached, often involving another person: _I'm meeting my advisor at 3pm on Thursday._
- **going to** expresses intention without necessarily being fixed yet: _I'm going to talk to my advisor sometime this week._

In practice, for near-future personal arrangements, native speakers often prefer present continuous over going to (_I'm flying to Lisbon on Friday_ sounds more natural than _I'm going to fly to Lisbon on Friday_).

---

## 4. Present simple for the future

Used almost exclusively for **timetabled, scheduled, or official** events — things that are fixed by a calendar, not by personal decision:

- _The train leaves at 9am._
- _The semester starts on August 3rd._
- _The registry office closes at 5pm._

⚠️ Common error: using present simple for personal plans that aren't officially scheduled. ~~"I go to the cinema tomorrow"~~ → _I'm going to the cinema tomorrow_ (present continuous, personal arrangement) — present simple would sound like you're reading from a fixed schedule, which is wrong here.

---

## 5. Future continuous (will be + -ing)

**Use 1 — action in progress at a specific future time:**

- _This time next week, I'll be sitting my C1 exam._

**Use 2 — a "matter of course" future** (something that will happen naturally, without deliberate intention — useful for polite questions):

- _Will you be using the car later?_ (neutral enquiry, not asking for permission — softer/more polite than "Are you going to use the car?")

---

## 6. Future perfect (will have + past participle)

Something that will be **completed before** a specific point in the future.

- _By the time you read this, I will have already left._
- _She will have finished the report by Friday._

Common trigger phrases: _by, by the time, before, in [time period] from now_.

---

## 7. Future perfect continuous (will have been + -ing)

Emphasises the **duration** of an action up to a future point, rather than its completion.

- _By December, I will have been studying English for three years._
- _When she graduates, she'll have been living in Niterói for six years._

**Future perfect vs. future perfect continuous** — the key difference:

|Future perfect|Future perfect continuous|
|---|---|
|Focus on completion/result|Focus on ongoing duration|
|_I'll have written the essay by 6pm._ (it will be done)|_I'll have been writing for three hours by 6pm._ (emphasis on how long)|

---

## 8. Future in the past

Used to talk about a plan or prediction made **at a point in the past** — often one that didn't happen or whose outcome is now known.

- **was/were going to**: _I was going to call you, but I forgot._ (plan that didn't happen)
- **would**: _She said she would help._ (future-from-past-perspective, common in reported speech — see the Reported Speech section of the main Study Guide)
- **was/were about to**: _I was about to leave when the phone rang._ (plan interrupted at the very last moment)

---

## 9. Time clauses — no future tense after when/as soon as/until/etc.

⚠️ This is a very high-frequency C1 error, and it trips up Portuguese speakers specifically because Portuguese _does_ use a future-like subjunctive in the equivalent clause.

After time conjunctions (**when, as soon as, until, before, after, once, by the time**), English uses **present simple** (or present perfect) to refer to future time — never _will_.

- ❌ _I'll call you when I will arrive._
- ✅ _I'll call you when I arrive._
- ❌ _She won't sign the contract until she will see the final version._
- ✅ _She won't sign the contract until she sees the final version._
- ✅ _As soon as I've finished the report, I'll send it to you._ (present perfect emphasises completion before the main action)

The _will_ only appears in the main clause, never in the time clause itself.

---

## 10. Quick reference — choosing the right form

|You want to express...|Use|
|---|---|
|A decision made right now|will|
|A promise, offer, spontaneous reaction|will|
|A plan decided earlier|going to|
|A prediction based on visible evidence|going to|
|A fixed personal arrangement (date/time set)|present continuous|
|A timetabled/official event|present simple|
|An action in progress at a future moment|future continuous|
|Something finished before a future point|future perfect|
|Duration of something up to a future point|future perfect continuous|
|A past plan (often unfulfilled)|was/were going to|
|The future inside a time clause (when/until/as soon as...)|present simple/perfect — never will|

---

## 11. Common errors to watch for (yours and generally)

1. Using **will** after **if/when/as soon as/until** — see §9.
2. Confusing **will** and **going to** for plans vs. spontaneous decisions — see §2.
3. Using present simple for personal (non-timetabled) plans instead of present continuous — see §4.
4. Forgetting **future perfect** needs "by/before + a point in time," not just any future reference.
5. Mixing up **future continuous** ("I'll be arriving") with **going to** ("I'm going to arrive") — future continuous is often more natural for polite/neutral statements about arrangements already in motion.
