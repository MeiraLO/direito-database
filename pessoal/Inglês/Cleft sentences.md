Cleft sentences split one idea into two clauses to shift emphasis onto a particular part of the sentence. Instead of saying something plainly, you "cleave" it apart to spotlight the piece you want to stress. They're common in C1-level writing and speaking, especially for adding emphasis or contrast in essays.

## It-clefts

Structure: **It + be + [emphasized element] + that/who + rest of sentence**

Take a plain sentence: _"Maria filed the petition yesterday."_

You can emphasize different parts:

- **It was Maria who** filed the petition yesterday. _(emphasizes who did it)_
- **It was the petition that** Maria filed yesterday. _(emphasizes what was filed)_
- **It was yesterday that** Maria filed the petition. _(emphasizes when)_

This is especially useful for correcting or contrasting: _"It wasn't the registry office that made the error — it was the notary."_

## Wh-clefts (pseudo-clefts)

Structure: **What/Where/Why + clause + be + [emphasized element]**

Plain sentence: _"I need more time to review the contract."_

- **What I need** is more time to review the contract.
- **What worries me** is the ambiguity in clause 3.
- **Where the problem lies** is in the payment terms.

These are great for topic sentences in opinion essays — they set up a clear focus before you elaborate.

## Reversed wh-clefts

You can also flip the order for a punchier effect:

- More time to review the contract **is what I need**.
- The ambiguity in clause 3 **is what worries me**.

## Why they matter for your writing

Cleft sentences are a strong way to hit the C1/C2 "range of complex structures" criterion on Cambridge writing tasks, since they show you can manipulate word order deliberately rather than just writing in default subject-verb-object patterns. They're also useful in formal/legal register — you'll notice **it-clefts** show up naturally in argumentative writing when you want to isolate blame, credit, or causation (e.g., _"It was the absence of a formal notification that invalidated the process"_).

One common mistake: don't overuse them. One or two per essay for emphasis reads as sophisticated; every sentence structured this way reads as unnatural.
