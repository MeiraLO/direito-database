# Exam Focus Predictions

## Cambridge C1 Advanced — Reading and Use of English (confirmed current format)

<cite index="9-1">The paper has eight parts: Part 1 Multiple Choice cloze, Part 2 Open Cloze, Part 3 Word Formation, Part 4 Key Word Transformation, Part 5 Multiple Choice reading, Part 6 Cross-Text Multiple Matching, Part 7 Gapped Text, and Part 8 Multiple Matching.</cite> <cite index="9-1">Parts 1–4 test grammar, vocabulary, collocations and phrases directly, while Parts 5–8 test reading comprehension.</cite> <cite index="4-1">The Writing paper has two tasks of 220–260 words each</cite>, and the whole exam runs across four papers testing reading/use of English, writing, listening and speaking.

### Where your topics land, part by part

|Cambridge part|What it tests|Your topics most at risk|
|---|---|---|
|**Part 2 (Open Cloze)**|Grammar words: prepositions, articles, conjunctions, linkers|despite/in spite of/although, comparative particles (as...as, the...the), reported-speech linkers|
|**Part 3 (Word Formation)**|Prefixes/suffixes from a root word|word formation, negative prefixes (ir-/il-/im-/dis-), noun/adjective/verb conversion|
|**Part 4 (Key Word Transformation)**|Rewriting a sentence using a given word, same meaning, 3–6 words|**this is the single biggest payoff area for you** — reported speech, conditionals, passives, cleft sentences, gerund/infinitive, wish/if only, and despite/in spite of all show up here constantly|
|**Part 1 (Multiple Choice cloze)**|Vocabulary, fixed phrases, collocations|less directly grammar, but participle-based idiomatic phrases appear|
|**Writing (Parts 1–2)**|Essay + choice of email/letter, proposal, report, review|cleft sentences and mixed conditionals for sophistication; passives for formal/impersonal register (reports); participle clauses to avoid short, choppy sentences|

**Bottom line:** if you only have time to master one thing before the exam, master **Key Word Transformation** technique — it directly rewards fluent conversion between active/passive, direct/reported speech, conditionals, and gerund/infinitive, which is exactly your "Things to fix" list.

## B2 First — same logic, slightly simpler ceiling

B2 First mirrors this structure at a lower level: Reading and Use of English also has a Word Formation part and a Key Word Transformation part, but the range of structures tested is narrower (expect more straightforward passives and reported speech, less mixed-conditional complexity, fewer cleft sentences). If you're deciding between B2 First and C1 Advanced, your quiz score above is a good proxy: scoring 45+/50 on the diagnostic suggests you're already past B2 territory and should prepare directly for C1 Advanced.

## IELTS — different test, same grammar underneath

IELTS doesn't have a discrete grammar paper — there's no cloze or word-formation task. Instead, grammatical range and accuracy is one of four scoring criteria in **Writing** and **Speaking**, assessed holistically rather than tested item-by-item. Practical implications for you:

- **Writing Task 2 (essay)**: examiners specifically reward a mix of complex structures — conditionals, passives, cleft sentences, participle clauses — used _accurately_ and _naturally_. A band 7+ script typically shows controlled use of at least 2–3 of your "fix" list topics per essay, not forced into every sentence.
- **Writing Task 1 (report/letter)**: passives are especially valuable here for describing data/processes impersonally ("it can be seen that...", "the figures are shown to have increased").
- **Speaking Part 3** (abstract discussion): hypothetical language (wish, if only, second/third conditionals) and past modals of deduction are natural fits for speculating and evaluating — exactly the kind of thing examiners are listening for to award higher grammatical range scores.
- Since IELTS has no isolated "fix the grammar" task, the practical prep move is different from Cambridge: don't drill transformations in isolation as much — instead, practise producing full paragraphs that _naturally_ contain 2nd/3rd conditionals, passives, and participle clauses, since that's how they're actually scored.

## Given your NPJ/legal-writing background — a genuine advantage

Legal and formal registers in Portuguese and English overlap heavily with exactly what these exams reward: passive voice, formal reporting verbs, cleft sentences for emphasis, and nominalisation (word formation). Your everyday work drafting formal correspondence to cartórios is, structurally, closer to Cambridge/IELTS formal writing than most candidates' daily English exposure — lean into that instinct rather than fighting it.
